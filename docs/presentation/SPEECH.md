# REEP — the launch speech

*For the person on stage. About nine minutes spoken. Two movements: the first
to the students, the second to the faculty. The refrain is "You are not your
marks. You are the distance you travel." The portal is the instrument, never
the subject; every touchpoint mentioned is something it actually does. Pause
where the lines break.*

---

## Part one — to the students

Jai Shri Gurudev.

I am not going to talk to you today about a portal. I am going to talk to you
about the person you are going to become in the next two years, and about the
one thing that decides it.

Not your marks. Not your college. Not your English, your accent, your family
name or your luck. The one thing that decides it is whether you grow a little,
honestly, every single day. And whether anyone can see it.

Let me tell you what growth is not. Growth is not a good day before the exam.
It is not a certificate you collected and forgot. It is not the feeling of
being ready. Growth is a distance. It is the gap between the person who walked
into this hall and the person who walks out of the interview room in your
final year. And a distance can be measured, if someone has the courage to mark
where they started.

So here is the refrain I want you to leave with:
**You are not your marks. You are the distance you travel.**

Now, how does a person actually travel that distance? Not with motivation;
motivation is weather, it comes and goes. With habits. Five of them. I will
give you all five, and I will tell you where each one lives, so you can start
tomorrow morning.

**The first habit is to know yourself honestly.** Most students spend two years
guessing what their teachers think of them. You will not guess. Your mentor
and the placement office write four lines about you: your strengths, your
weaknesses, your opportunities, your challenges, each with a name and a date.
Reading your weaknesses in someone's handwriting takes courage. Read them
anyway. Everyone who ever became excellent at anything started by admitting
what they were bad at. The four lines are not a judgement. They are the map.

**The second habit is to own your hours.** Twenty-four hours a day, and you
will account for every one of them: sleep, travel, lectures, coursework,
skilling. The day must add up before you can submit it. Do you know why? Not
because anyone wants to police you. Because the single biggest difference
between a professional and a student is that a professional knows where the
time went. Half an hour you cannot explain is the most honest lesson you will
receive all week. Learn to look at it without flinching.

**The third habit is to prove, not claim.** There is a difference between
attending a course and learning something, and the world outside will find
that difference in the first ten minutes of your first job. So here, nothing
is claimed. Forty-eight skills, and every one must be earned: you do the work,
you upload the proof, and a teacher who knows you checks it and puts their
name behind it. When that badge lights up, it is not a sticker. It is
integrity made visible. Build a record that a stranger can trust, and you will
never have to sell yourself again; the record will do it.

**The fourth habit is to practise the fear away.** The interview is the most
frightening twenty minutes of a young person's life, and the reason is simple:
most of you have never done one. So do a hundred. There is an interviewer on
this portal that speaks, listens, probes, and scores you at the end, and it
does not care that it is eleven at night in your hostel room. Stumble in
private. Stumble eight times in a day if you must. Every stumble that happens
here is one that does not happen in front of the panel. Confidence is not a
personality. Confidence is rehearsal.

**The fifth habit is to ask for help and answer feedback with work.** Your
mentor is one button away. Ask for the meeting. Then read what they write
afterwards, because every note is written to you, not about you, and go back
with the work done. A student who does that six times in a semester becomes
someone a mentor fights for. Humility is not weakness. Humility is how fast
you learn.

Five habits. Know yourself. Own your hours. Prove, don't claim. Practise the
fear away. Answer feedback with work. None of them needs talent. All of them
need honesty.

And here is where the technology matters, and only here. Your seniors did
these things, if they did them at all, with a notice board, a WhatsApp group
and a rumour, and the first honest feedback most of them ever got came from
the interviewer who rejected them. The technology has moved so that your
feedback comes in hours, not at the end of the semester. So that your
strengths are written where you can read them. So that your practice
interview is available at midnight. So that every job on the board tells you
exactly which line you are missing, and you can go and fix the line instead of
fearing the list. The tools changed so that the loop of growth, attempt,
feedback, attempt again, can run every day instead of once a year. That is
the whole point of modern learning: not a lecture you attend, a loop you run.

One more thing about growth, and it is the one I care about most.

The only comparison that helps you is with the person you were yesterday. On
this portal the leaderboard that matters is not the one for points. It is the
one for growth: how far you have moved from your own starting line. The
student we celebrate here is not the one who arrived the strongest. It is the
one who travelled the furthest. Some of you walked in behind. Good. You have
the longest distance to show, and distance is what the world pays for.

Now, honesty like this is only possible where there is trust, so let me tell
you what this college has done to deserve yours. Your record stays yours;
it does not leave our machines without your permission, and that is a rule in
the software, not a line on a poster. Nothing about your practice interviews
is kept until you have read, in plain words, what will be kept, and agreed.
Only your mentor and the placement office can see you; nobody appoints
themselves. And nothing is ever said about you that is not also said to you.
That is what makes it safe to be honest here. And honesty is where every
distance begins.

So this is my ask. Do not aim to be placed. Aim to become a person who is
impossible not to place: self-aware, disciplined, provable, rehearsed,
teachable. The offer letter is a side effect of that person. And that person
does not stop at the first job. The habits you build here are the ones that
carry you through the second job, the third, the one you cannot imagine yet.

You are not your marks. You are the distance you travel.

Start tomorrow. Mark where you are. Then move.

## Part two — to the faculty

Now to my colleagues, because a student can only travel as far as the people
around them let them.

The college is handing each of you a small group of young people and the
whole picture of their year: their attendance, their work, their claims,
their practice interviews, their requests for help. I want to be clear about
what that picture is for. It is for growing people. Not for ranking them.

Growing people looks like this. You see attendance slip in the week it
happens, and you ask why, that week. A certificate arrives in your queue, and
within two working days you either light the badge or write one clear sentence
about what is missing, so the student can act. A student's four lines are
blank, and you fill them, because a nineteen-year-old with no strengths
written down has been given nothing to build on. A practice interview names
the same weakness three times, and that becomes your next meeting. Small,
fast, specific. That is the method: feedback that is seen is feedback that
works, and growth we can point to is worth more than growth we assume.

And there are things this picture is never for, and I will say them slowly.

Never for ranking a student by their circumstances. A dip in attendance, a
leave, a weak baseline: things to help with, never to hold against anyone.
Assess the work. Never the situation.

Never for counting what was not measured. A missing score shows as a dash,
not a zero, on purpose. A number is where a conversation starts, not where a
verdict ends.

Never for sharing. What you see stays with your group. No screenshots, no
forwarding, no pasting a student's record into some outside tool to save ten
minutes. The rule that student data does not leave our machines binds us as
much as the software, and every screen you hold, you hold because the office
granted it, with a reason, on a trail. That is what makes their trust in us
rational.

Never for writing what you would not say to the student's face. They read
every note. Write as a mentor, in words they can act on tomorrow.

Never for favours. A badge is verified because the proof is genuine, not
because you like the student, and a refusal carries a reason they can fix.
Same evidence, same criteria, same timeline, for everyone. That is fair
assessment, and the trail records every decision so that fairness can be
shown, not claimed.

And never for surveillance. Recordings exist only with consent, and the time
sheet is the student's mirror, not our stick.

Colleagues, no generation of teachers before us could see a student's year in
time to change it. We can. The measure of us will not be how much we saw. It
will be how far our students travelled because we saw it, and acted, fairly,
in time.

Guide them. Assess them fairly. Protect what they trusted us with.

You are not your marks. You are the distance you travel. Let us make sure
every one of them travels far.

Thank you. Jai Shri Gurudev.
